# 1. Wrong vs. Right First Project

The single biggest risk in publishing your first book isn't formatting, keywords, or marketing. It's **choosing a first project too big for a beginner to finish well**. Most first-book stalls happen before page one of the manuscript — at the idea stage, or ten thousand words into a scope that keeps growing.

This chapter helps you talk yourself out of the wrong first book and into the right one.

## The wrong first projects

These are the books that *feel* like good ideas and consistently sink first-timers. We know because we considered — and abandoned — several of them on the way to *The Massive AI Manual*.

### 1. The Wikipedia-sized handbook on a giant topic

"Everything about world history," "the complete guide to cooking," "a detailed deep-dive into the US Navy." A topic that never ends guarantees the manuscript never ends. Nonfiction giant-handbooks also mean: thousands of fact-checking obligations, copyright-clearance questions for images, and no clear "reader's problem solved."

A beginner asks too much and ships nothing.

### 2. The topic with a fast expiration date

"Everything about the hot new model released last week" — dangerous. Cut-off dates and rapidly shifting tool interfaces mean your interior will feel out of date months after launch. Durable topics outlast UI screenshots and version numbers.

### 3. The fiction series opener

A 90,000-word novel is a *marathon* for a first book; a series locks in more. If your real goal is to learn KDP publishing mechanics, fiction offers a beautiful-but-steep learning curve.

### 4. The topic outside a real reader's reach

If you can't describe one person who would buy it and one problem it solves, stop.

### 5. The book that needs other people's property

Books built around copyrighted quotes, trademark logos, expensive licenses, complicated image credits — likely permission-hell for a first project.

## The right first project

- **One reader with one real problem.** "I know AI exists but I don't know what to actually try."
- **A topic you already know deeply enough to teach in plain English** — teaching clarity, with no jargon, is your real qualification.
- **A topic with multi-year lifespan** — no dependence on a single product staying stable.
- **A length you can finish** — a plain, useful 150–250 page manuscript in normal language beats a stalled 600-page monster.
- **Something you'd recommend to a friend** — your honest standard.

## The Massive AI Manual: what we learned

Real lessons from our ground-truth-first-book experience:

- **We cut two projects before ever writing.** They were textbook wrong-shape projects — scope, freshness, or audience mismatch. Knowing what you're *not* writing is a legitimate milestone.
- **We wrote for one specific reader:** someone curious about AI + agents/tools, but not technical. Every manuscript decision — examples, glossary, "like a friend would" refinement — flowed from that reader description.
- **We wrote plain, not thin.** Applying "plain English, step by step, never condescending" kept progress fast.
- **We shipped a real interior deliberately** — via the page-by-page process in the next chapter.

## How to decide in one sitting

Write your project decision down before starting the manuscript:

- **Reader:** One sentence. Who is this *for*, specifically?
- **Problem:** One sentence. What can the reader *do* after finishing?
- **Shape:** Rough page budget (drafting comfortably at release?) with a hard stop/not-beyond list.
- **Down-to-earth lifespan:** Is this still true and useful in 3 years?
- **One thing you'll cut if the writing takes too long.** (Write it *down now*. Scope creep is easiest to kill when you've pre-identified the juiciest cut.)

Discussing — not "committing," just discussing — with one honest friend or family member. If you can't describe your first chapter to them in two minutes, your scope is wrong.

## Pitfalls to avoid

1. **Tool-thinking.** Don't plan to build the perfect word processor before you start. Google Docs or Word has one. Ship.
2. **Clock watching.** "This year everyone will already know it" — false urgency. Do it as long as it takes to build right.
3. **Sneaky self-doubt** — "My topic is already covered." So what? Can you serve a specific reader more helpfully, with a specific voice, than what exists? That's enough for one good book.
