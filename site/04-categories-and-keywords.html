4. Categories and Keywords Without Tricks

KDP asks you for up to three categories and seven keywords when you set up your book. These fields have attracted an entire folklore industry — "category hacking," keyword stuffing, hunting for obscure subcategories with low competition. This chapter explains what these fields actually do, and how to use them in a way that gets your book found by the right reader without trading away your integrity or inviting a policy takedown.

What these fields actually do

  • Categories help Amazon's browse system show your book alongside similar books. Readers find you there via browsing, and finding-adjacent books matters for long-tail discovery.
  • Keywords are the strings Amazon's search engine matches against queries (along with your title, subtitle, and description). Good keywords make your book appear when someone searches in your book's topic, using that topic's real language.

Neither field makes a weak book sell. Both make a good book easier to find. That's their whole job, and it's an honest job.

Categories: match, don't manipulate

  1. Pick the category that truly matches the content first. What's the book's subject? A beginner AI manual belongs in categories about AI/computers/how-to technology — not in "Business Motivation" because that category has more browsers.
  2. Three slots: fill them, but keep them honest. Use all three only if all three are genuinely accurate. If your book fits one category cleanly and a second one partly, use the second; don't strain for the third.
  3. Don't chase the smallest category to game a ranking. Adding categories chosen purely to hit "top 100 in an obscure niche" is a known trick, does not help sales substantially, and monetizes nothing. A good book in a correct category serves the reader who's browsing there.
  4. Fix inaccuracy when you find it. Amazon support can update categories if the AI-assigned ones don't fit. Ask honestly.

Keywords: phrases, not lists of words

Each of the seven keyword slots can hold a phrase up to 50 characters — and a phrase is what you want. Keywords work best when they're the actual query a real reader would type into Amazon.

How to find them honestly

  1. Start with the reader's problem. For an AI-for-beginners book: "what is an AI agent," "AI for non-technical people," "how to use ChatGPT," "beginner's guide to artificial intelligence."
  2. Look at the autocomplete suggestions in Amazon's search bar — those are real queries real people type. They're also a fair, public source of inspiration.
  3. Think in the reader's plain language, not your own. Readers search "AI that works on my computer," not "locally-hosted inference on consumer GPUs." Use the phrase in their vocabulary.
  4. Don't repeat your title. Title, subtitle, and description are already indexed. Keywords should add additional related terms.
  5. No trademark names in a keyword to attract searchers you then redirect. Descriptive mention of a product inside the book is normal; keyword slots loaded with trademarked terms to catch searches violates KDP policy and Amazon trademark policy (documented in KDP's keyword guidelines — read them; they're short).

What NOT to do (this list is the "without tricks" part)

  • No keyword stuffing (repeating the same word in multiple slots to spam relevance).
  • No "free", "bestseller", "best" in keywords — forbidden terms include subjective claims and price claims. KDP documents a short list of prohibited keyword content. KDP can suppress your book for keyword policy violations, and a suppression during launch week is a self-inflicted wound.
  • No other-author names or book titles in your metadata.
  • No misleading claims ("shares secrets Amazon doesn't want you to know"). Nothing that inflates content you don't have.

Your description: the honest sales page

Your description sells the book with the truth. Basics that actually work:

  1. Open with the reader's problem, in one or two sentences. "AI is everywhere and nobody explained it to you in plain English. This book does."
  2. Say what's inside, concretely. Actual chapter-level promises. "Everything about AI" promises nothing; "How agents work, where to start with local AI, what MCP is in plain English" promises something.
  3. Name the reader it's not for. This is disarming and filters well. "If you're a developer wanting GPU-tuning code, this isn't your book." Honest scoping builds trust and reduces refunds and bad reviews.
  4. Format for skimming. Short paragraphs, a short bullet list of what's covered. The description shows on mobile; dense text loses readers.
  5. No fake urgency, no fake scarcity, no "only today." Nothing in your honest description requires a technique, and every trick you don't use is trust you do keep.

What we did for The Massive AI Manual

  • We chose the AI/computers and how-to nonfiction categories honestly and used a plain-language keyword set around beginner queries.
  • We wrote the description from the reader's side and explicitly said who the book is not for.
  • We deliberately skipped every strategy that requires buying services, farming reviews, or category manipulation. The result: fewer sales at launch, and reviews from real readers who got exactly what we said they'd get. Long-term, that's the compounding asset: a record of reviews that match the description.

Review integrity: one rule

Never buy, trade, incentivize, or fabricate reviews. Not "rarely" — never. Ask real readers (or the small audience you actually have, if any) to read the book and write what they honestly think. If you get no early reviews, so be it: launch anyway. A book with honest reviews arrives slowly and stays; a book built on incentivized reviews gets removed and takes a piece of your account with it.

Quick self-check

  • [ ] Categories all match the actual content.
  • [ ] All seven keywords are phrases a real reader would type, none trademark-abusing, none claim-based.
  • [ ] Description states the problem, the concrete contents, and who the book isn't for.
  • [ ] No stuffing, no claims, no borrowed names.
  • [ ] Nothing in the whole setup would embarrass you if printed in the book's own copyright page back matter.
