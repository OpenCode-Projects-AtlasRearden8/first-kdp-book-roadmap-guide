3. Pricing Honestly

Pricing is where beginners make the most avoidable mistake — not by choosing the wrong number, but by choosing a number before doing the math. This chapter gives you the math, then gives you a decision process that ends with a number you can defend to yourself and live with for years.

Nothing here is hype. No "launch at 99 cents and watch the velocity algorithm kick in." Just arithmetic and a few honest principles.

**The numbers below are the values we worked with for The Massive AI Manual — Amazon's KDP royalty schedule and US printing-cost formula. KDP documents the current authoritative numbers on its royalty help page and its printing-cost help page, and they do change. Recompute with today's numbers before you set your list price; the method here is durable.

The two numbers that determine your royalty

1. The royalty rate: 35% or 70%

For Kindle ebooks sold in most marketplaces you choose (each royalty zone set on the pricing page at the time of writing), there are two tiers:

Tier List price range (US, at the time of writing) Royalty
70% $2.99 – $9.99 70% minus a delivery fee
35% Anything (including below $2.99 and above $9.99) 35%, no delivery fee

Key consequences, stated plainly:

  • Pricing at $2.99 or above opens the 70% tier. Pricing at $2.98 or below hands you the 35% rate. That's a huge cliff, and it sits exactly at the $2.99 line, which is why you see $2.99 and $9.99 everywhere.
  • Pricing above $9.99 drops you to 35% for the ebook. For a long, substantial reference manual this can be a hard choice. Some reference books do fine at $14.99 at the 35% rate ($5.25 per sale) versus $9.99 at 70% ($6.99 per sale). Do your math, for your book, at the current rates.
  • The delivery fee in the 70% tier is charged per megabyte of the ebook file (a documented $0.15/MB in the US tier at the time of writing). A lean, text-based manual with a small file keeps this near zero. If your ebook is stuffed with large images, the delivery fee can quietly eat a real percentage of a 70% royalty. Optimize your images; it pays.

2. The printing cost (paperback)

For paperbacks, KDP deducts a printing cost before your royalty, and the formula is public. At the time of writing, for US black-ink paperbacks, the cost is roughly:

fixed cost + (page count × per-page cost)

Ask KDP's calculator with your actual trim size and page count for your exact print cost — the fixed component differs by trim size and ink type. For example, a 200-page black-ink 6"×9" paperback was around a few dollars to print, meaning a $14.99 list price earned the author roughly $5–7 per sale at the fixed 60% list-price rate KDP pays on paperbacks (minus printing).

Do this once, in KDP's calculator, with real numbers. Write the per-sale royalty down. Everything else in this chapter hangs off that figure.

Worked example (illustrative, with round numbers)

Suppose your book ends up like this (purely illustrative numbers you would replace with your own):

  • Ebook: 210-page manuscript, mostly text, ~0.8 MB file.
  • List price $9.99 → 70% tier → $9.99 × 0.70 = $6.99, minus delivery fee (tiny: ~$0.12) → ≈ $6.87 per sale
  • List price $14.99 → 35% tier → ≈ $5.25 per sale
  • Paperback: 210 pages, 6"×9", black ink.
  • Printing cost (from KDP's calculator) ≈ $3.65
  • List price $14.99 → KDP pays 60% of list = $8.99, minus $3.65 printing → ≈ $5.34 per sale

What this example shows: at these numbers, the paperback and the "expensive" ebook earn similar per-sale amounts — a common surprise. It also shows why a $2.99 ebook would be a mistake for this book (≈ $1.95 per sale, and rewardingly cheap forever).

Pricing honestly: five principles

  1. Price for the reader's value, not your feelings. A question that matters: what is it worth to the specific reader you chose in Chapter 1? Not what it cost you to make. Not what your competitor's book costs them.
  2. Choose a price you can leave alone. This is the "low-stress" part. A price that bounces with your mood or with a slow sales day is a tax on your attention. A price chosen for the long run lets you spend launch energy on the book and the reader, not the dial.
  3. Do not inflate-then-"discount" forever. The trick of listing high and running permanent 99-cent sales erodes reader trust and trains nobody. If $4.99 is the right price, list $4.99.
  4. Don't underprice to "help" the reader. A too-low price doesn't get you more sales in most durable early-first-book situations; it loses money you'll never recover and signals "skim." The $2.99 cliff exists for a reason; be careful when a $0.99 or free promotion is involved.
  5. The royalty you want per unit, times units you realistically sell — that's your revenue. Revenue forecasts built on "if I just reach the top 100 in category X" are gambling. A forecast built on "50 copies/month at $6.87 = $343.50/month" is plan-able, and modest numbers like that are exactly the honest starting point for most first books.

Pricing puts free into perspective

KDP has mechanisms (like KDP Select's free days, and the 35% tier's minimum prices) that let you give copies away. A few honest thoughts:

  • Giving away copies has real uses — early reviewer readers, charity, a genuine taste of The Massive AI Manual series quality. Used deliberately, they're fine.
  • As a strategy, "lose money on volume" is a beginner trap. Free readers are rarely buyers of your next thing at the rate promo folklore promises.
  • If you want a free sample, make the Look Inside and a sample chapter do that work — no contract to manage, no artificial scarcity game.

Your pricing worksheet

Do this once, with your real numbers from KDP's calculator:

  1. Ebook: file size in MB, price candidates, delivery fee at each → per-sale royalty.
  2. Paperback: page count, trim size, printing cost → per-sale royalty at 2–3 candidate list prices.
  3. Break-even: if you have any out-of-pocket costs (an editor, a proof copy, cover art), how many sales to cover them?
  4. Sanity check: at your candidate price, would you buy this book as the reader you wrote for? Would you be comfortable if it never changed?

Not sure? Pick the middle of your defensible range. You can change price later — KDP lets you, and it takes effect within a day or two. Changing it weekly is the only real pricing mistake. This chapter is education, not financial advice. Pricing decisions, business elections, and tax treatment are yours and a professional's to make.
